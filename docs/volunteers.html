Volunteers plant a great many of Canada’s urban trees. The question this section answers is whether they are filling a gap left by underfunded municipal programs — or whether they turn up precisely where those programs are strongest.

Published findings

Martin, A.J.F., Conway, T.M., & Fleming, A. (2026). Volunteer engagement in Canadian urban forestry – A neoliberal fix or a complement to urban forestry staff? Urban Forestry & Urban Greening, 124, 129618.

Just over half of the municipalities that answered the census — 52.6% — work with volunteers on tree planting, tree care, or other urban forestry activities on public land. Scaled to all 343 urban municipalities in Canada, we estimate that about 165 of them engage volunteers, or roughly 48% nationally. That is somewhat below the 65% reported in the United States.

The scale of it

Among municipalities that work with volunteers, the median engages 40 volunteers a year contributing about 150 hours — though the spread is wide, with the middle half of municipalities reporting between 20 and 250 volunteers. Sixty-seven municipalities reported a combined 72,791 volunteers, and sixty-three reported 183,320 hours.

Extending those figures to non-responding municipalities through a two-stage model gives a national picture: roughly 105,000 volunteers contributing about 379,000 hours annually. Individual Canadian volunteers give fewer hours than their American counterparts — a municipality-level average of 6.1 hours per volunteer each year, against 9.8 in the US — but Canadian municipalities engage volunteers at a comparable or slightly higher rate per head of population.

Who volunteers

More municipalities work with individual residents than with any single type of organization. Where people volunteer through a group, local environmental organizations and school or youth groups are the most common routes. Municipalities typically work with several types at once.

What volunteers do

Volunteer effort is concentrated in planting, and specifically in the lower-risk settings. Naturalization plantings — in ravines, riparian corridors and wooded areas — and park tree planting dominate. Street tree planting, which carries traffic and utility hazards, is far less common, and routine maintenance is rarer still. The median municipality reported two activity types.

Notably, tree inventory work — a common volunteer activity in the United States — is uncommon in Canada, even though most Canadian municipalities maintain or are developing an inventory.

Training

Nearly nine in ten municipalities that work with volunteers provide training, a higher rate than the 79% reported in the US. Tree planting training is almost universal among them, matching what volunteers are actually asked to do. Safety training is offered at roughly half that rate, which may reflect how carefully volunteer involvement is kept to lower-hazard tasks.

A complement, not a substitute

The central question here is whether volunteers stand in for professional staff and public funding. The census says they do not. Municipalities with larger populations, bigger urban forestry budgets and more staff were more likely to engage volunteers, engaged more of them, offered a wider range of training, and partnered with a broader set of organizations. Municipalities that described their own budget as inadequate were no more likely to lean on volunteers.

Volunteer engagement is enabled by strong municipal programs rather than driven by institutional shortfalls.

Climate and landscape shape the picture too. Municipalities with longer frost-free seasons were considerably more likely to work with volunteers, while wetter places reported fewer partner organizations and fewer activity types — growing seasons and weather windows constrain when outdoor volunteer events are feasible. Municipalities with more canopy overall supported a wider range of volunteer activities, likely because parks and naturalized areas lend themselves to stewardship work.

Taken together, this is the first national evidence that volunteering in Canadian urban forestry works as a complement to municipal capacity and a route to community engagement — not as a cheap replacement for trained staff or adequate funding.
