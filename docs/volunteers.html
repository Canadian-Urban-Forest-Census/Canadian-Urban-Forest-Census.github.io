Volunteers are an active part of Canadian urban forestry, contributing to tree planting, tree care, public outreach, and other stewardship activities. The 2025 Canadian Urban Forest Census provides the first national picture of who volunteers, what they do, and how municipalities support their involvement.

Published findings

Martin, A.J.F., Conway, T.M., & Fleming, A. (2026). Volunteer engagement in Canadian urban forestry – A neoliberal fix or a complement to urban forestry staff? Urban Forestry & Urban Greening, 124, 129618.

Across Canada, we estimate that 48.1% of urban municipalities engage volunteers, representing approximately 105,400 volunteers contributing 379,100 hours each year.

The scale of it

Among municipalities that engaged volunteers, the median program involved 40 volunteers contributing 150 hours annually. Municipalities with larger urban forestry budgets and more staff tended to engage more volunteers and record more volunteer hours. Larger municipalities also engaged more volunteers, while higher-density municipalities recorded more volunteer hours.

Across Canada, we estimate that approximately 105,400 volunteers contribute 379,100 hours to municipal urban forestry each year. On a per-capita basis, Canadian municipalities averaged about 529 volunteers per 100,000 residents, compared with 322 in the United States. However, Canadian volunteers contributed fewer hours per person: an average of 6.1 hours annually compared with 9.82 hours among responding U.S. municipalities.

Who volunteers

Individual residents were the most commonly engaged participants, reported by 57.1% of municipalities that worked with volunteers. Municipalities also frequently partnered with local environmental groups (52.9%), schools and youth organizations (48.6%), non-profit or non-governmental organizations (37.1%), and private corporations (34.3%). All other organization types were reported by fewer than one-third of municipalities.

What volunteers do

Volunteer activities were concentrated in lower-risk stewardship work, particularly tree planting. Naturalization planting — that is, planting in ravines, riparian areas, or forested areas — was by far the most common activity, reported by 82.9% of municipalities that engaged volunteers. Park tree planting followed at 70.0%, while street tree planting (21.4%) and community education and engagement (20.0%) were much less common. All other activities were reported by fewer than one-fifth of municipalities.

The emphasis on planting in parks and naturalized areas likely reflects both the accessibility and relatively low risk of these activities. Street tree planting and maintenance work were much less commonly supported by volunteers. Keeping higher-risk work in the hands of trained professionals helps protect volunteers while allowing them to contribute to municipal urban forestry programs.

Training

Training was common among municipalities that worked with volunteers: 88.9% provided some form of training. Tree planting training was by far the most common, offered by 83.3% of municipalities, followed by safety training (47.2%) and tree identification (25.0%). All other forms of training were offered by fewer than one-fifth of municipalities, and just eight municipalities reported providing no volunteer training.

Canadian municipalities reported training volunteers more frequently than municipalities in the United States, where 79% provided training. The strong emphasis on tree planting mirrors the activities volunteers most often perform. Safety training, however, was offered by fewer than half of municipalities, which may partly reflect the concentration of volunteer work in lower-risk activities.

A complement, not a substitute

Municipalities with larger urban forestry budgets and more staff engaged more volunteers, recorded more volunteer hours, worked with a wider range of partner organizations, and provided more types of training. By contrast, perceived budget inadequacy was not associated with greater volunteer involvement. Together, these findings suggest that volunteer programs depend on the staff time, resources, and organizational capacity needed to support them rather than compensating for inadequate staffing or funding.

Strong municipal programs create the capacity to involve volunteers, while volunteers broaden opportunities for community participation and connection to the urban forest.

These findings challenge interpretations of volunteerism as primarily a response to austerity. Instead, volunteerism in Canadian urban forestry appears to complement professional staff and public investment while adding value to urban forestry programs. That value may include increasing public awareness of urban trees and strengthening community stewardship.
