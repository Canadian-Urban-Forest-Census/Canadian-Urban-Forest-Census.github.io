Across Canada, towns and cities plant, prune, inspect and remove the trees that line their streets and fill their parks. How that work is organised — who does it, what it costs, and what gets recorded — varies enormously from one municipality to the next, and until recently there was no national picture of it.

The Canadian Urban Forest Census (in French, Recensement des forêts urbaines canadiennes) is a bilingual survey of municipal urban forestry programs across the country. In 2025 we invited every urban municipality in Canada to describe its tree care program: the staff who run it, the budget behind it, the inventories and assessments that inform it, the contractors and volunteers who support it, and the plans and policies that guide it. One hundred and thirty-seven municipalities responded — a 40% response rate.

Why a census

National surveys of this kind give municipalities something they rarely have: a way to compare their own program against others of a similar size. They also show researchers and funders where the gaps are. Similar surveys have been run in the United States since the 1970s, and more recently in Ireland, New Zealand, Sweden and the United Kingdom. Canada’s previous efforts were more limited in scope, often restricted to larger municipalities or to those already on an existing contact list.

This census took a different approach to eligibility. Rather than setting a population floor, we began from Statistics Canada’s definition of an urban area — a continuously built-up area of at least 1,000 people with a density of at least 400 people per square kilometre — and applied it to all 5,161 census subdivisions in the country. That produced 343 eligible municipalities in every province, from four each in Prince Edward Island and Newfoundland and Labrador to 88 in Québec. Small towns are represented alongside the largest cities.

What the survey asked

The questionnaire ran to 106 questions across eight sections, covering community and staff, operations, budget, tree management, inventory and assessment, contractors, and volunteers and partnerships. It was reviewed by municipal urban forestry managers in British Columbia, Ontario and Québec before it went out, and their feedback shaped it — most notably by adding a set of questions specific to street trees, since responsibility for trees in natural areas often sits with an entirely different department and makes municipality-to-municipality comparison misleading.

Full detail on the survey instrument, eligibility, and how the survey was distributed is on the Methods page.
