Urban forest planning is the process of establishing what a municipality wants its urban forest to become and how it will get there. It involves understanding the current state of the urban forest, setting goals for its future, developing strategies to achieve those goals, and monitoring progress over time. Planning helps municipalities make decisions about where to plant trees, how to manage existing trees, and how to allocate limited resources.

Urban forest planning is often described as a four-stage process, shown below.

Stage 1What do we have?

Urban forest goals and strategies are best established based on the extent and distribution of the current tree population. Yet less than half of municipalities knew their tree canopy cover or the number of municipally owned trees, and even fewer knew the number of street trees in their municipality.

The greater the population of the municipality, the more likely they were to know their canopy cover and number of trees. Municipalities with greater canopy cover were also more likely to know their canopy cover, but less likely to know how many municipally owned trees they had.

Among municipalities that knew their canopy cover, the median was 25%. Their figures were generally close to our independent estimates from high-resolution canopy height mapping, with a median difference of 2.1 percentage points. However, some differed by more than 50 percentage points in either direction, largely among municipalities with extensive riparian or contiguous forests, which may not be considered in canopy cover estimates by these municipalities.

More on how municipalities track their trees is available on the Tree Inventories page.

Stage 2What do we want?

Municipalities can set goals for increasing tree canopy cover, such as reaching 25% canopy cover by 2050, or for planting a certain number of trees, such as planting 100,000 trees by 2050. Canopy cover goals were more common than planting goals, although more than half of municipalities had neither. Among municipalities with canopy or planting goals, 37.3% had also set goals for individual neighbourhoods based on their existing canopy cover.

Municipalities with greater populations were more likely to have either type of goal. Municipalities with greater existing canopy cover were also more likely to have a canopy cover goal. Each additional 10 percentage points of canopy cover was associated with about 74% higher odds of having a canopy cover goal.

How goals are set

Recommendations from consultants were the most common basis for setting canopy cover and planting goals, followed by the availability of space for new trees and recommendations from academic research. Local considerations, including municipal budgets, political leaders’ preferences, and equitable canopy distribution, were less frequently considered. Public opinion was among the least commonly used factors.

Most municipalities considered only one or two factors when setting their goals, although municipalities with greater populations tended to consider more factors. About a quarter (27%) relied exclusively on recommendations from consultants or academic research, without considering local factors such as available planting space, municipal budgets, or public opinion.

Trees as infrastructure

Just over half of municipalities (51.9%) reported using their urban forest to help meet stormwater management, public health, or air quality goals. This was more common among municipalities with canopy cover or planting goals (70.7%) than among those without them (37.1%). More populous municipalities and those with greater canopy cover were also more likely to use their urban forest for these purposes.

Stage 3How do we get there?

Municipalities use urban forestry strategies and master plans to guide the management of their trees. Nearly half of participating municipalities had a written strategy or master plan, and another three in ten were developing one. Just over a quarter had neither.

Municipalities with greater populations were more likely to have a plan in place or under development. Each doubling of municipal population was associated with about 73% higher odds of being at a more advanced stage of planning, from having no plan to developing one or having one in place.

Who develops the plan

Municipal staff were involved in developing nearly every urban forestry plan, and consultants contributed to almost two-thirds. Local government representatives, such as city councillors, were involved in fewer than half. Participation from volunteers, non-profit organizations, and Indigenous communities was uncommon.

Most plans involved two or three types of participants. The number of participant types was not associated with municipal population, density, growth, income, or canopy cover.

Who sets policy

City council committees and community boards were the most commonly reported bodies involved in establishing tree management policy. Parks boards and municipal staff were less frequently involved, while dedicated tree boards and urban forestry commissions were uncommon.

Stage 4Are we making progress?

Of the 57 municipalities with canopy cover or planting goals that answered this question, just under half (49.1%) reported being on track to meet them. Progress towards these goals was not associated with municipal population, density, growth, income, or canopy cover, nor with the number of factors considered when setting the goals.

Municipalities that considered consultant recommendations when setting their goals were less likely to report being on track (39.4%) than those that did not (66.7%). This difference was not explained by municipal population. However, with only 54 municipalities included in the comparison, the result fell just short of statistical significance and should be interpreted cautiously.

Only half of municipalities with canopy cover or planting goals reported being on track to meet them.
