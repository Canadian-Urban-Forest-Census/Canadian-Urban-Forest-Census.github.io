Urban forest planning is the process of establishing what a municipality wants its urban forest to become and how it will get there. It involves understanding the current state of the urban forest, setting goals for its future, developing strategies to achieve those goals, and monitoring progress over time. Planning helps municipalities make decisions about where to plant trees, how to manage existing trees, and how to allocate limited resources. Urban forest planning is often described as a four-stage process, shown below.

Stage 1What do we have?

Setting and tracking goals depends on knowing what is there to begin with. Fewer than half of municipalities (41.8%) could report their tree canopy cover, 39.1% knew how many municipally owned trees they had, and fewer than a quarter (23.1%) knew how many street trees they had.

Larger municipalities were more likely to know all three. Municipalities with more canopy were more likely to know their canopy cover, but less likely to know how many municipally owned trees they had. Where municipalities did report a canopy cover figure (median 25%), it was generally close to our independent estimate from high-resolution canopy height mapping (median difference 2.1 percentage points), but the largest differences exceeded 50 percentage points in either direction. More on how municipalities track their trees is on the Tree Inventories page.

Stage 2What do we want?

Canopy cover goals, such as 25% canopy cover by 2050, were more common than tree planting goals, such as planting 100,000 trees by 2050. Of the 133 municipalities that answered, 38.3% had a canopy goal, 18.0% had a planting goal, and 12.0% had both. More than half (55.6%) had neither.

Larger municipalities were more likely to have either type of goal. Municipalities that already had more canopy were also more likely to set a canopy goal: every 10 percentage points of existing canopy cover was associated with about 74% higher odds of having one. Among municipalities with goals, 37.3% had set goals for specific neighbourhoods based on their existing canopy.

How goals are set

Recommendations from consultants were by far the most common basis for canopy and planting goals, used by 60.7% of municipalities with goals. Around two in five considered the plantable space available for new trees (41.1%) or drew on research and academic recommendations (39.3%). Local considerations such as budget (30.4%), political leaders’ preferences (28.6%), and equitable canopy distribution (26.8%) were less common, and few municipalities used public opinion to set their goal (12.5%).

Most municipalities based their goals on only one or two factors (the median was two), although larger municipalities tended to consider more. About a quarter (26.8%) relied only on outside expertise, from consultants, research, or both, without weighing any local factors such as plantable space, budget, or public opinion.

Trees as infrastructure

Just over half of municipalities (51.9%) use their urban forest to help meet stormwater, public health, or air quality goals. This was much more common among municipalities with a canopy or planting goal (70.7%) than among those without one (37.1%). Larger municipalities and those with more canopy were also more likely to use their urban forest this way.

Stage 3How do we get there?

Of the 137 participating municipalities, 43.8% have a written strategy or master plan for urban forestry or arboriculture, and a further 29.2% are developing one. Just over a quarter (27.0%) have neither. Larger municipalities were further along: each doubling in population was associated with about 73% higher odds of being at a more advanced stage, from no plan to developing one to having one in place.

Who develops the plan

Municipal staff were involved in developing nearly every plan (92.9%), and nearly two-thirds of municipalities (64.6%) also brought in consultants. Local government, such as city council, took part in 44.4% of plans. Wider involvement was rare: volunteers (11.1%), non-profit organizations (8.1%), and Indigenous communities (5.1%) each took part in only a small share of plans. Most plans were developed by two or three types of participants, and this did not vary with municipal size, density, growth, income, or canopy cover.

Who sets policy

Policy for tree management was most often set with input from a city council committee or community board (77.0%). Parks boards (24.6%) and staff (20.6%) were much less common, and dedicated tree boards or urban forestry commissions were rare.

Stage 4Are we making progress?

Of the 57 municipalities with goals that answered the question, just under half (49.1%) said they were on track to meet them. Being on track was not related to municipal size, density, growth, income, or canopy cover, nor to how many factors were considered when setting the goal.

Municipalities whose goals drew on consultant recommendations were less often on track (39.4%) than those whose goals did not (66.7%). This was not explained by municipal size, but with only 54 municipalities to compare, the difference fell just short of statistical significance and should be treated with caution.

Half of the municipalities that have set canopy or planting goals say they are not on track to meet them.
