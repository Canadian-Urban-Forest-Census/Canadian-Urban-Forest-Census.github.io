How municipalities manage their trees day to day: which departments are responsible, how street trees are planted, pruned, and removed, and how municipalities handle tree risk, pests, wood residue, and storms. Plans and canopy targets are covered on Urban Forest Planning, and bylaws on Tree Protection Bylaws.

Most participating municipalities planted more street trees than they removed in 2024, but street tree management is still largely reactive. Half prune only as needed, and nearly half say responding to residents’ complaints is their main way of finding risky trees. Fewer than half have an emergency response system that includes trees, and 42.6% say storms have stretched their pruning cycle.

Who manages municipal trees

Nearly all participating municipalities (97.8%) carried out some urban forestry or tree care in 2024, and 97.1% had someone, whether an employee, volunteer, or consultant, overseeing the care of trees on public land. That responsibility was often shared between departments. Parks and recreation (72.1%) and public works (68.4%) were most often involved in managing municipal trees, followed by planning (43.4%) and dedicated forestry or urban forestry units (37.5%).

Primary responsibility was most often held by parks and recreation (34.3%), public works (28.5%), or a dedicated forestry or urban forestry unit (27.0%). Larger municipalities were much more likely to give primary responsibility to a forestry unit: each doubling in population was associated with nearly three times the odds. Municipalities with more canopy were somewhat less likely to do so.

The department responsible for municipal trees almost always cared for both street trees and park trees (97.1% each), and most (78.8%) were also responsible for trees in natural areas such as ravines and riparian areas. Staff numbers, training, and credentials are covered on the Staffing and Contracting page.

Street tree work in 2024

The typical municipality planted 50 street trees, pruned 100, and removed 21 in 2024, and responded to 60 street tree service requests. More than half treated no street trees at all for pests or diseases.

Population was the main driver of how much work was done: each doubling in population was associated with roughly twice as many trees planted, pruned, and removed, and about three times as many service requests. Pest and disease treatments followed a different pattern, rising with the amount of street tree canopy and falling with population density.

Planting versus removal

Across participating municipalities, 109,217 street trees were planted in 2024 and 71,735 removed. Significantly more municipalities planted more trees than they removed (56.9%) than the reverse (32.1%).

Planting

Most street tree planting replaced trees that had been removed: 83.2% of municipalities did this, and it was the main approach for nearly half (47.4%). Planting through capital projects (70.8%) and by developers (65.0%) was also common. Half of municipalities planted in response to residents’ requests, but almost none relied on it as their main approach. One in ten municipalities did not plant street trees.

Among municipalities that plant street trees, most combined planting at the municipality’s direction with planting on request (62.3%). Nearly a quarter planted at the municipality’s direction but let property owners decline, an “opt-out” approach (23.0%). Very few planted mainly when owners asked, an “opt-in” approach (4.9%).

Nearly two-thirds of municipalities (64.9%) provided formative care for young trees in their first three to five years after planting. More than half (54.7%) planted trees as green stormwater infrastructure, while schoolyard (25.6%) and highway (18.8%) planting were less common.

Pruning

Half of municipalities pruned street trees only as needed (50.0%), and 42.6% had a regular inspection and pruning cycle. A small number pruned only after storms or other emergencies, or did not prune street trees at all.

Among municipalities with a regular cycle, most pruned continuously throughout the year (84.5%). The median cycle was six years, against a desired median of five. Most were already on the cycle they wanted (56.4%), but 41.8% wanted a shorter one, and almost none wanted a longer one. Larger municipalities tended to be further behind their desired cycle, and denser municipalities closer to it, although this rests on only 53 municipalities.

Removals

Nearly every municipality removed trees that were declining or dead (125 of 128). Measured by the number of trees, declining or dead trees (34.9%) and tree risk (33.9%) each accounted for about a third of all street trees removed. Disease and insect problems (9.7%) and development (9.6%) each accounted for about a tenth, and infrastructure conflicts and storm damage for most of the rest. Removals at a homeowner’s request were cited by 51 municipalities but made up less than 1% of trees removed.

Tree risk

Most municipalities (62.5%) regularly carried out tree risk management, and 79.4% routinely inspected and removed high-risk trees on public property. Yet only 27.2% had a written tree risk management policy or plan. Among municipalities that rated tree risk, most used the ISA Tree Risk Assessment Qualification (TRAQ) method (62.7%), while 20.5% used their own system and 15.7% used none. Larger municipalities were more likely to use TRAQ.

Nearly every municipality inspected trees in response to resident complaints (94.2%), and for nearly half (48.2%) this was the main way risky trees were found. Proactive approaches, such as routine risk inspection programs or car-based surveys, were the main approach for only about a third.

Pests and diseases

Municipalities managed exotic invasive pests, such as emerald ash borer, more actively than native pests. Ongoing management was much more common for invasive pests (39.0%) than for native ones (23.5%), and 30.9% did no native pest management at all. Nearly a third of municipalities (30.1%) managed invasive pests more intensively than native ones, while few did the reverse. Most municipalities (71.8%) routinely inspected and removed diseased trees on public property.

Wood residue

When a public tree is removed, the wood is most often turned into mulch for landscaping (77.6%). Many municipalities also sent wood to landfill (41.8%) or cut it into firewood (36.6%). Higher-value uses, such as furniture, flooring, art, or lumber, were uncommon, and a quarter of municipalities (25.6%) reported urban wood utilization as an activity. Three-quarters (74.8%) tracked neither the volume of wood nor where it went.

Storms

Fewer than half of municipalities (46.0%) had an emergency response system that included trees, and in about half (51.9%) no one in the tree program had training in an emergency management system. Only 19.3% said their emergency manager held storm meetings that discussed trees along public roads.

Storms are already affecting routine work: 42.6% of municipalities said more storm response calls had extended their inspection or pruning cycle. In their own words, respondents described storms using up budgets and diverting crews from planned pruning, creating backlogs. Several also noted the reverse: where they had pruned proactively, storm damage had dropped.

Reactive tree care and storm response compete for the same crews and budgets. Several municipalities found that proactive pruning reduced storm damage.
