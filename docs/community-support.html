Most urban trees grow on private land, so municipal urban forestry depends on residents, landowners, and partners as much as on municipal staff. The 2025 Canadian Urban Forest Census asked municipalities how they educate and engage residents, what help they give with private trees, and how much support and understanding they see for urban trees in their communities.

Nearly three-quarters of participating municipalities (72.3%) provide some form of tree education to residents, and two-thirds (67.2%) offer technical or financial help with private trees. But only a third (34.3%) agree that they regularly give residents opportunities to take part in urban forest planning, and that engagement, more than education, is what goes with stronger community support.

Education

Maintaining a webpage dedicated to urban forestry was the most common form of education, reported by 45.0% of municipalities. Somewhat fewer provided residents with general tree education (42.7%) or education about the value of trees (36.1%), and about one in five (21.4%) provided tree education to K–12 students. Larger municipalities offered more types of education: each doubling in population was associated with about 52% higher odds of offering more activities.

Engagement

Engagement goes beyond providing information: it gives residents a say in how urban forestry objectives are set and carried out. Municipalities were split on whether they regularly provide these opportunities. About a third agreed (34.3%), a third disagreed (35.0%), and the rest were neutral.

Support versus understanding

Respondents saw broad support for street trees across their communities. More than four in five agreed that street trees were supported by residents (81.8%), elected officials (83.9%), and municipal administration (86.9%), and 72.1% said the same of operations staff.

Understanding of what street trees are worth lagged behind that support. The gap was widest for residents: 81.8% of respondents agreed that residents support street trees, but only 55.2% agreed that residents understand their value. The gap was smaller for elected officials (83.9% versus 65.7%) and administration (86.9% versus 73.5%), and all three differences were statistically significant. Only among operations staff were support and understanding about the same (72.1% versus 73.7%).

Support for urban forestry goals

Support for the municipality’s own urban forestry goals was weaker and varied more between groups. Just over half of respondents agreed that the private tree care industry (57.4%) and residents (52.6%) support these goals, and 46.7% agreed that neighbouring municipalities and regional groups cooperate to support them. Large landowners stood out: only 19.0% agreed that they support municipal urban forestry goals. Within municipalities themselves, only 22.6% agreed that all business units work toward common urban forestry goals.

Respondents generally viewed the private tree care industry positively. Besides supporting municipal goals, more than half of respondents (54.4%) agreed that it operates with high professional standards, while 16.2% disagreed.

What goes with stronger support

Municipalities that agreed they regularly provide engagement opportunities also reported stronger support for their urban forestry goals from residents, large landowners, and the private tree care industry. The link was strongest for residents. Engagement also went with greater perceived understanding of street tree value among all four groups, and with stronger community support for private tree protection bylaws.

Education did not show the same pattern. Neither the number nor the type of educational activities was related to perceived support for urban forestry goals or to understanding of street tree value. Technical and financial assistance was also unrelated to support for urban forestry goals and to residents’ understanding of street tree value.

Giving residents a regular say in urban forestry appears to matter more for building support and understanding than providing information alone.

Technical and financial assistance

Two-thirds of municipalities (67.2%) offered at least one form of technical or financial assistance with trees. The most common were wood and brush recycling sites for residents (40.5%) and technical advice on maintaining private trees (38.9%). Financial help with removing infested or diseased private trees was rare (9.9%). Unlike bylaws and education, the number of assistance programs a municipality offered was not significantly related to its population, density, growth, or canopy cover.

Public land versus private land

Municipalities were far more active in managing risky and unhealthy trees on their own land than on private property. Most routinely inspected and removed high-risk trees (79.4%) and diseased trees (71.8%) on public land, but few inspected and required the removal of hazardous (11.5%) or infested and diseased (17.6%) trees on private land. Both differences were statistically significant. Diseased or hazardous private trees can still threaten public infrastructure and spread pests to public trees, so this gap matters beyond individual properties.

Assistance did not consistently go hand in hand with regulation. Municipalities with private tree protection bylaws offered slightly fewer assistance programs than those without, even though such bylaws place responsibilities on residents who may lack the money or expertise to care for large trees.
