The 2025 Canadian Urban Forest Census was developed with input from municipal urban forestry practitioners and researchers. The survey was distributed to Canada's 343 urban municipalities between August and October 2025 using email invitations and mailed postcards. A total of 137 municipalities participated, representing 40% of those eligible.

The census followed four steps: designing the survey instrument, identifying which municipalities were eligible and finding their contact details, distributing the survey, and evaluating how well it performed.

Designing the survey

We began development of the survey using a the long-form questionnaire developed for the 2025 Urban and Community Forest Census in the United States. To adapt the survey for Canadian municipalities, we replaced American government programs, standards, and terminology with their Canadian equivalents. The initial draft contained 92 questions.

Three municipal urban forestry managers — one each in British Columbia, Ontario and Québec — reviewed the initial draft, which led to two changes. First, because responsibility for trees in natural areas often falls to different departments, questions about all municipally owned trees may not be comparable between municipalities. In response, we added 15 questions specific to street trees. Second, the original budget section asked for more detail than municipalities typically record, itemising budgets and expenses that appear as a single line in an annual report. In response, we removed two questions, simplifying the budget section.

Seven urban forestry researchers from across Canada then peer reviewed the revised survey, and a final question on local research needs and priorities was added, bringing the total survey to 106 questions. The survey ultimately comprised eight sections: community and staff, operations profile, budget, tree management profile, inventory and assessment, contractors, volunteers and partnerships, and concluding comments.

The survey was written in English and translated into French for Québec and New Brunswick, with the translation reviewed by a bilingual research assistant and peer reviewed by a francophone researcher and a francophone practitioner. It was delivered through Qualtrics, an online survey platform.

Who was eligible

Rather than setting our own population threshold, we adopted Statistics Canada’s definition of an urban area:

Of the 5,161 census subdivisions in the 2021 Census, 362 met this definition. Sixteen of these were Indigenous communities, which we excluded because they operate under governance structures different from those of municipalities. That left 346 urban, non-Indigenous subdivisions.

Three adjustments were required to reflect current municipal boundaries:

  1. Turner Valley and Black Diamond, Alberta amalgamated into a single new municipality, Diamond Valley. Updated population figures for Diamond Valley still met the inclusion criteria, so it was included.
  2. Petit-Rocher, New Brunswick amalgamated with neighbouring villages and unincorporated areas to form Belle-Baie. With an updated population density of 25.72 people per square kilometre, the new municipality no longer met the density threshold and was excluded.
  3. Lloydminster sits on the Alberta–Saskatchewan border and is counted as two separate subdivisions, one in each province. Both census subdivisions met the urban definition, and we merged the population data for the two subdivisions. For analysis we treated it as Albertan, since its city hall moved from Saskatchewan to Alberta in 2000.

After making these adjustments, there were 343 eligible municipalities. They are spread across all ten provinces, ranging from four each in Prince Edward Island and Newfoundland and Labrador to 88 in Québec. None of the three territories contained a municipality meeting the urban definition.

Finding contacts

Every municipality received an email to its general inbox. Beyond that we looked for named people, working down a hierarchy of sources:

  1. Municipal websites, searching for forestry, parks, horticulture, environment or planning departments and their senior staff.
  2. Google and LinkedIn, searching for urban foresters, arborists and parks staff by municipality.
  3. Public sector salary disclosure records, where available through provincial records.
  4. Phoning the municipality’s information line and asking for the relevant department.

We prioritised individual work addresses, then departmental, then administrative contacts. We capped contact at four addresses per municipality to avoid excessively emailing any one municipality

Distributing the survey

The survey opened on August 6, 2025 and closed on October 17, 2025. The initial mass email was sent out on August 6, with subsequent follow up emails on Septemebr 5 and October 2 to municipalities that had not yet responded. In the two follow up emails, we reported the participate rate to date. Emails went out in the official language of the province, with bilingual emails to New Brunswick.

Alongside email, each municipality received a single postcard with a QR code to the survey. We also posted bilingual notices to CANUFNET, a national urban forest mailing list run by Tree Canada, and on LinkedIn.

Survey responses

Of the responses, 133 came through the emailed link and four through the postcard QR codes. Municipalities where we had found at least one individual work address had 3.57 times the odds of participating compared with those reached only through a general inbox. Similarly, municipalities contacted via departmental addresses had 2.69 times the odds of participating as those those contacted thorugh a general inbox. There was no significant difference in response rates based on the number of email addresses for the municipality.

Among the 137 responses, 59% came from staff in management roles, while 40% came from staff in non-manamagement positions. Manager was the single most common title, followed by director and supervisor. Common non-management position titles included arborist, urban forestry technician, coordinator, and foreperson.

Checking for bias

To see whether responding municipalities differ systematically from those that did not respond, we compared the two groups on population, population density, land area and canopy cover, with canopy cover derived from a one-metre global canopy height model. Participating municipalities were significantly larger in both land area and population. Population density and canopy cover showed no significant difference. We also compared early and late responders, and found no significant differences on any of the four measures.

In short, larger municipalities were more likely to answer, but responders and non-responders are not distinguishable by their population density or canopy cover.
