This page discusses how the 2025 census was developed, who it was distributed to, and how we checked whether the 137 participating municipalities were reflective of the non-participating municipalities.

Published methods paper

Martin, A.J.F., Conway, T.M., Bacon, E.S., Almas, A., Hauer, R.J., King, G., Koeser, A.K., & Ngan, A. (2026). Conducting a national urban forest management survey – Insights from the 2025 Canadian Urban Forest Census. Urban Forestry & Urban Greening, 121, 129459.

The census followed four steps: designing the survey instrument, identifying which municipalities were eligible and finding their contact details, distributing the survey, and evaluating how well it performed.

Designing the survey

We began from the long-form questionnaire used by the 2025 Urban and Community Forest Census in the United States, adapting its questions to the Canadian context by replacing American government programs, standards and terminology with their Canadian equivalents. The draft ran to 92 questions.

Three municipal urban forestry managers — one each in British Columbia, Ontario and Québec — then reviewed it. Two changes came out of that review. First, because responsibility for trees in natural areas frequently sits with a different business unit, questions about all municipally owned trees were not comparable between municipalities; we added 15 questions dealing specifically with street trees. Second, the original budget section asked for more granularity than municipalities typically record, itemising budgets and expenses that appear as a single line in an annual report, so two questions were removed to simplify the budget questions.

Seven urban forestry researchers from across Canada then peer reviewed the revised survey, and a final question on local research needs and priorities was added, bringing it to 106 questions in eight sections: your community and staff, operations profile, budget, tree management profile, inventory and assessment, contractors, volunteers and partnerships, and concluding comments.

The survey was written in English and translated into French for Québec and New Brunswick, with the translation reviewed by a bilingual research assistant and peer reviewed by a francophone researcher and a francophone practitioner. It was delivered online through Qualtrics rather than on paper, which lowered costs and allowed repeated contact without wasting printed surveys.

Who was eligible

Rather than setting our own population threshold, we adopted Statistics Canada’s definition of an urban area:

Applying that definition to all 5,161 census subdivisions from the 2021 Census left 362 that qualified as urban. Of those, 16 were Indigenous communities, which we excluded because they operate under governance structures different from those of municipalities. That left 346 urban, non-Indigenous subdivisions.

Three of those 346 subdivisions needed reconciling against current municipal boundaries:

  1. Turner Valley and Black Diamond, Alberta amalgamated into a single new municipality, Diamond Valley, which still met the urban criteria and was included as one.
  2. Petit-Rocher, New Brunswick amalgamated with neighbouring villages and unincorporated areas to form Belle-Baie. At 25.72 people per square kilometre, the new municipality no longer met the density threshold and was excluded.
  3. Lloydminster sits on the Alberta–Saskatchewan border and appears as two separate subdivisions, one in each province. Both met the urban definition, and we merged them into the single municipality they are. For analysis we treated it as Albertan, since its city hall moved from the Saskatchewan side to the Alberta side in 2000.

That produced a final list of 343 eligible municipalities. They are spread across all ten provinces, ranging from four each in Prince Edward Island and Newfoundland and Labrador to 88 in Québec. None of the three territories contained a municipality meeting the urban definition.

Finding contacts

Every municipality received an email to its general inbox. Beyond that we looked for named people, working down a hierarchy of sources:

  1. Municipal websites, searching for forestry, parks, horticulture, environment or planning departments and their senior staff.
  2. Google and LinkedIn, searching for urban foresters, arborists and parks staff by municipality.
  3. Public sector salary disclosure records, where the province publishes them.
  4. Phoning the municipality’s information line and asking for the relevant department.

We prioritised individual work addresses, then departmental ones, then administrative contacts, and capped contact at four addresses per municipality to avoid flooding any one organisation.

Distributing the survey

The survey opened on 6 August 2025 and closed on 17 October. Three mass emails went out — on 6 August, 5 September and 2 October — each subsequent one only to municipalities that had not yet responded, and each reporting the participation rate so far. Emails went out in the official language of the province, with bilingual emails to New Brunswick.

Alongside email, each municipality received a single postcard carrying a QR code, addressed to the department most closely tied to urban forestry. We also posted bilingual notices to CANUFNET, the national urban forest mailing list run by Tree Canada, and on LinkedIn.

What worked

Of the responses, 133 came through the emailed link and four through the postcard QR codes. Municipalities where we had found at least one individual work address had 3.57 times the odds of participating compared with those reached only through a general inbox; departmental addresses gave 2.69 times the odds. Adding more addresses per municipality made no significant difference — what mattered was reaching a specific person or department, not sending more mail.

Notably, only about a third of the responses came from someone we had emailed directly. The rest were completed by colleagues the survey had been forwarded to, which suggests reaching the right department matters as much as reaching the right individual.

Who answered

We asked each respondent for their job title. Just under 60% of the 137 responses came from people in management roles, and 40% from staff in non-management positions.

Checking for bias

To see whether responding municipalities differ systematically from those that did not respond, we compared the two groups on population, population density, land area and canopy cover, with canopy cover derived from a one-metre global canopy height model. Participating municipalities were significantly larger in both land area and population. Population density and canopy cover showed no significant difference. We also compared early and late responders, and found no significant differences on any of the four measures.

In short, larger municipalities were more likely to answer, but responders and non-responders are not distinguishable by their population density or canopy cover.
