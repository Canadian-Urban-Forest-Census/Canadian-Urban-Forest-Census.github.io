Contractors carry out a large share of the tree care in Canadian municipalities, from planting to pruning and removal. The 2025 Canadian Urban Forest Census asked municipalities whether they use contractors, what work they hire them for, and what standards and qualifications they expect contractors to bring.

Nearly all participating municipalities (97.1%) use paid contractors for at least some of their arboriculture work, and 87.1% of those use contractors for street trees. Removal and pruning are the most commonly contracted activities, while just over half of municipalities contract street tree planting.

Who uses contractors

Of the 136 municipalities that answered the question, 132 (97.1%) use paid contractors for at least some arboriculture activities, such as tree planting, pruning, or removal. Among those, 115 (87.1%) use contractors for work on street trees specifically. Contracting is therefore close to universal in Canadian urban forestry, and the more useful questions are what work is contracted and what is expected of the contractors who do it.

What contractors do

Among municipalities that contract street tree work, removal (92.2%) and pruning (84.3%) were by far the most commonly contracted activities. Just over half (58.3%) contracted tree planting, and just under half (47.0%) contracted plant health care, such as pesticide injections or spraying. Few municipalities (7.0%) used contractors for education and outreach.

Removal and pruning often involve large trees, specialized equipment, and significant risk, which may explain why they are contracted so widely. Larger municipalities were more likely to contract tree planting, and municipalities that planted more street trees were also more likely to contract that work.

What municipalities ask of contractors

Municipalities can set formal requirements for contracted work, such as following industry standards, and can give hiring preference to contractors with particular qualifications. Hiring preference for ISA Certified Arborists was by far the most common expectation, reported by 85.8% of municipalities that use contractors. Formal requirements to follow a work standard were less common: 41.6% required ISA Best Management Practices and 35.4% required ANSI A300 Standards.

Matching standards to the work

Planting-specific standards followed the planting work. Among municipalities that contract tree planting, 41.5% required contractors to use the Canadian Nursery Stock Standard, which sets specifications for the quality of nursery trees. In Québec, where the BNQ 0605-100 and 200 standards cover landscaping and tree maintenance work, every municipality that required BNQ standards also contracted tree planting.

General work standards, such as ISA Best Management Practices and ANSI A300, were required more often by municipalities that contract plant health care, which tend to contract a wider range of work overall. This suggests that some municipalities set more formal expectations across all of their contracts, rather than tailoring requirements to each activity.

Contract specifications are one of the main ways a municipality can shape the quality of contracted tree care. Naming the relevant standard for each type of work, such as a nursery stock standard for planting, makes those expectations explicit.
