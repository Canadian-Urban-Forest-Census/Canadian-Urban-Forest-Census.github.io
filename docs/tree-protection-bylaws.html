Tree protection bylaws set legal obligations for keeping trees on public and private land, usually by restricting the removal of trees above a minimum size. The 2025 Canadian Urban Forest Census asked municipalities which bylaws and related provisions they have, and how well those bylaws work in practice.

Nearly three-quarters of participating municipalities (73.5%) have a bylaw protecting street trees, and more than half (57.7%) have one protecting trees on private property. Yet only about half of municipalities with these bylaws agree that they are actively enforced, and most report that enforcement is not sufficiently staffed.

Bylaws and provisions

Street tree bylaws were significantly more common than private tree bylaws. Of the 136 municipalities that answered both questions, nearly half (48.5%) had both types, a quarter (25.0%) protected street trees only, and very few (8.8%) protected private trees only. Overall, 17.5% of municipalities had neither a street nor a private tree bylaw.

Municipalities can also protect canopy on private land through other regulatory provisions. The most common were requiring residents to obtain a permit before removing a tree (37.6%) and requiring landscape plans for permit approval (36.1%). About one in five set minimum canopy requirements for new development sites (20.3%) or gave special protection to significant, specimen, or heritage trees (18.8%). Overall, 14.1% of municipalities had neither a bylaw nor any of these provisions.

Which municipalities have them

Larger municipalities were more likely to have a street tree bylaw: each doubling in population was associated with about 43% higher odds of having one. Private tree bylaws followed a different pattern. They were more likely in denser municipalities (about 11% higher odds for every additional 100 people per km²) and in municipalities with more existing canopy (about 3% higher odds per percentage point of canopy cover), but were not significantly related to population.

The number of private-land provisions a municipality had (0 to 4) also rose with population, population density, and canopy cover. Population change between 2016 and 2021 was not associated with any of these regulations.

How well bylaws work

Municipalities with a bylaw rated five statements about how it works in practice. Ratings were broadly similar for street and private tree bylaws. About half agreed that their bylaws were actively enforced (51.5% for street trees and 50.0% for private trees). The clearest challenges were staffing and penalties: more than half disagreed that enforcement was sufficiently staffed (55.6% and 59.0%), and nearly half disagreed that penalties were sufficiently punitive (46.5% and 49.4%). Views on community support for enforcement and on whether offences were increasing each year were more mixed, with many neutral responses.

Among the 65 municipalities with both types of bylaw, none of the differences between their street and private tree ratings were statistically significant. The closest was community support: some respondents who were neutral about support for their street tree bylaw disagreed that their private tree bylaw had community support.

Community support and enforcement

Where respondents saw stronger community support for their bylaws, they also tended to see enforcement as more active and better staffed. This held for both street and private tree bylaws, and the relationships were stronger for private trees. For private tree bylaws, stronger community support also went with seeing penalties as sufficiently punitive. In contrast, community support was not related to how many private-land provisions a municipality had, and perceptions of increasing offences were largely unrelated to enforcement, apart from a weak link with insufficient staffing for street tree bylaws.

Adopting a bylaw does not by itself ensure it is implemented. Enforcement capacity and community support appear to rise and fall together.

Bylaws alongside education and assistance

Bylaws are one of three broad ways municipalities can protect and care for trees, alongside community education and engagement, and technical and financial assistance for residents. Municipalities with street or private tree bylaws offered more educational activities than those without, and municipalities with more private-land provisions also offered more education. However, municipalities with private tree bylaws offered slightly fewer technical and financial assistance programs, such as advice on maintaining private trees or help paying to remove diseased ones. This suggests that regulation and practical support for residents do not always go together.

Engagement may help close that gap. Municipalities that agreed they regularly provide opportunities for resident engagement also reported stronger community support for their private tree bylaws. Education and assistance were not linked to community support for bylaws. More on education, engagement, and assistance is on the Community Support page.
