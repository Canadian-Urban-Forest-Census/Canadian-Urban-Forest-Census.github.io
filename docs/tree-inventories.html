A tree inventory is any record a municipality keeps of its trees. Data from tree inventories can be used to guide species selection, identify planting locations, coordiante tree maintenance, and monitor change over time.

Published findings

Martin, A.J.F., Conway, T.M., Olson, L.G., & Bacon, E.S. (2026). Urban tree inventories in Canadian municipalities. Journal of Forestry. Advance online publication.

Read the paper (DOI)

Open access. The figures on this page are drawn from that paper.

Among the 135 municipalities that responded to the tree inventory section of the Census, 77% reported having a tree inventory in place or in progress. This is an increase from previous decades: 49% of Canadian municipalities had an inventory in 1998, and 67% in 2014.

The rise is probably steeper than those numbers suggest. Our survey included 48 municipalities with populations between 1,000 and 10,000, whereas the 2014 survey excluded communities under 10,000 — and smaller municipalities are much less likely to have a tree inventory.

How current are they

Having an inventory is different than it reflecting the current tree population. Only about a quarter of municipalities described theirs as up to date, while a third were in the process of reinventorying their trees.

Municipalities with larger land areas were substantially more likely to have a tree inventory, as was the municipality having an urban forestry budget. Meanwhile, more complete, up-to-date inventories were more common in municipalities with larger land area and higher population density, with additional strong associations with larger urban forestry budgets and higher staffing levels.

Municipalities' initial tree inventories were completed anywhere between 1950 and 2025, with a median of 2014. Among municipalities currently conducting their first inventory, completion is expected between 2025 and 2033. Where inventories are maintained, half of municipalities update them daily or weekly as work is carried out, while another sixth update only to record a planting or a removal.

How they are built

Field-based partial tree inventories are the most common. That is, an inventory of all street trees or all park trees, where streets or parks are a "stratum." Remote sensing is now used by nearly half of municipalities, almost always complementing rather than replacing a field-based inventory. Most municipalities use multiple approaches.

Collection has gone digital. Handheld devices — phones and tablets — are the most common tool at 76%, followed by GPS and GIS receivers at 51%. Pen and paper persists in about a quarter of municipalities, though only 12.5% rely on it exclusively. Nine in ten inventories are computerized or digitized, up from 43% a decade ago.

Municipal staff do most of the work, contributing in 91% of municipalities, followed by consultants in 46%. Volunteers and non-profits appear in under 3% each — a striking contrast with the United States, and consistent with what the volunteer findings show about where volunteer effort actually goes.

Which trees get counted

Inventories follow municipal responsibility. Street and park trees are almost universally covered. Natural areas appear in under half, and privately owned trees in under a third — and notably, using remote sensing did not meaningfully broaden that coverage.

What gets recorded

The commonly collected fields are the ones operations need: where the tree is, what it is, how big it is, and what condition it is in.

Two details matter for anyone hoping to combine these datasets. Common names are recorded far more often than scientific names, and species codes appear in under a quarter of inventories — which makes cross-municipal comparison harder than it needs to be. On the other hand, 82% now link records to GPS or GIS coordinates, ahead of street addresses at 55%, giving trees a stable spatial reference that survives renumbering and re-surveying.

What they are used for

Inventory use is overwhelmingly operational and planning-oriented. Assessment and policy applications trail well behind.

Most municipalities put their inventory to several uses, averaging 4.7, but more than one in five reported using it for a single purpose only. The number of uses rose with budget and staffing, not with the mere existence of a budget.

Support for tree inventories needs to extend beyond creating them, to the staff, maintenance and data-management capacity that keeps them current and useful.

What this suggests

Inventories appear to follow a trajectory — adoption, development, maintenance, use — with institutional capacity mattering more at each later stage. A budget line is enough to get an inventory started; keeping it current and getting value from it depends on the size of that budget and the people behind it.

The paper sets out four practices worth adopting: manage the data digitally, use geographic coordinates as the primary spatial reference, record accepted scientific names alongside common ones, and archive past inventory data rather than overwriting it. Together these make an inventory useful not just for this year's pruning schedule but for monitoring change over decades, and for comparison across municipalities.
