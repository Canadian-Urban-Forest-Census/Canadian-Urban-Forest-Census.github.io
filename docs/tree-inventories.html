Urban tree inventories provide municipalities with a record of their urban forest and are a foundational tool for tree planning, management, and monitoring. They can be used to understand the distribution and composition of urban trees, identify planting locations, and support day-to-day arboricultural operations. When maintained over time, they can also be used to monitor tree growth, mortality, and the outcomes of management activities.

Published findings

Martin, A.J.F., Conway, T.M., Olson, L.G., & Bacon, E.S. (2026). Urban tree inventories in Canadian municipalities. Journal of Forestry. Advance online publication.

Among the 135 municipalities that responded to the tree inventory portion of the 2025 Canadian Urban Forest Census, 77% reported having an existing inventory or one currently in development. Approximately half had completed their first inventory in 2014 or later, while a further 26 municipalities were conducting their first inventory at the time of the survey.

Tree inventories have become increasingly common in Canadian municipalities. In 1998, 49% of municipalities were reported to have an inventory1, increasing to 67% in 20142 and 77% in the 2025 Census. This increase may be greater than these figures suggest, as the 2014 survey excluded municipalities with populations below 10,000, while the 2025 Census included them. Smaller municipalities are less likely to have a tree inventory.

How current are they

Having a tree inventory does not necessarily mean that it is complete or current. One-third of responding municipalities were developing or redeveloping an inventory, while 27% reported having an up-to-date inventory. A further 15% had an outdated inventory, and a small number had an incomplete inventory with no plans to continue its development.

Maintaining a current and useful inventory requires continued investment in staff, maintenance, and data management. More current and complete inventories were associated with larger urban forestry budgets and greater staffing levels. Much like tree planting, where investment is required not only to plant a tree but also to maintain it after establishment, tree inventories require investment both to establish them and to keep them current and useful over time.

Municipalities completed their first tree inventories between 1950 and 2025, with a median completion year of 2014. Among municipalities conducting their first inventory at the time of the Census, completion was expected between 2025 and 2033. Where inventories are maintained, half of municipalities update them daily or weekly as work is performed, while another 17% update records when trees are planted or removed.

How they are built

There is no single approach to conducting a tree inventory. Field-based partial inventories were the most common, reported by 65% of municipalities. These inventories record all trees within a defined part of the urban forest, such as all municipal street trees or all municipal park trees. By comparison, one-quarter of municipalities used sample-based inventories, which record only a sample of the tree population.

Remote sensing, including lidar and satellite imagery, was used by 48% of municipalities. Most municipalities using remote sensing also maintained field-based inventories, reflecting the different information provided by the two approaches. Remote sensing can provide broad spatial coverage and canopy information, while field inventories can record detailed information on individual trees. More than half of municipalities used multiple inventory methods, averaging 1.9 approaches per municipality.

In the field, data were most commonly collected using handheld devices such as smartphones and tablets, used by 76% of municipalities. GPS or GIS receivers were used by 51%, while pen and paper remained in use among 27%. Overall, 91% of tree inventories were computerized or digitized, a substantial increase from the 43% reported a decade earlier.

Municipal staff conducted tree inventories in 91% of municipalities, with consultants or contractors also contributing in 46%. Interns contributed in 14%, while universities and colleges, volunteers, non-profit organizations, and other levels of government were much less frequently involved.

Volunteers contributed to inventories in fewer than 3% of municipalities, a notable contrast with the United States, where volunteer involvement in tree inventories is much more common. While community engagement in urban forestry is often desirable, tree inventories require the collection of detailed data used for management and monitoring, where professional data collection is often preferable.

Which trees get counted

Tree inventories were overwhelmingly focused on municipally managed trees. Almost every municipality with an inventory included street trees (99%), while 95% included park trees. Natural areas such as woodlots, greenbelts, and riparian areas were inventoried by 43% of municipalities, while only 29% included some or all privately owned trees.

This emphasis reflects the operational role of municipal tree inventories. Streets and parks contain trees for which municipalities have direct management responsibilities, making detailed information on these trees particularly valuable for day-to-day arboricultural operations.

What gets recorded

At their core, Canadian municipal tree inventories focus on a relatively consistent set of tree and site characteristics. The four most commonly recorded are tree location, species identification, diameter at breast height, and tree condition. Other information includes planting locations, maintenance needs, insect and disease problems, work orders, stumps, risk assessments, and site conditions.

Tree location was the most commonly recorded attribute, collected by 96% of municipalities. GPS or GIS coordinates were used by 82% of municipalities, compared with 55% using street addresses, 36% using parcel data, and 34% linking trees to other municipal infrastructure layers. Geographic coordinates provide a relatively stable reference for individual trees through successive inventories and make it easier to integrate tree records with other municipal spatial data.

Species common names were recorded more frequently than scientific names, at 90% and 71% of municipalities, respectively, while species codes were used by only 23%. Ideally, both common and scientific names are recorded. Scientific names provide standardized nomenclature and can be linked to maintained taxonomic authorities, helping to identify errors and keep inventory records aligned with accepted taxonomy.

What they are used for

Although tree inventories can support a wide range of planning, policy, monitoring, and research applications, Canadian municipalities primarily use their inventories for practical arboricultural operations. Identifying tree planting locations was the most common use (84%), while scheduling tree pruning and informing species selection were each reported by 62% of municipalities.

Most municipalities were getting more than one use from their inventories. Municipalities reported an average of 4.7 uses, and 77% used their inventory for multiple purposes. However, 23% reported using their inventory for only a single purpose. Inventories are clearly serving their traditional role as operational tools, but their use for broader policy, planning, and monitoring remains less common.

Expanding these applications does not necessarily require collecting more information. In some cases, the greater need may be staff expertise, geospatial infrastructure, and integration of tree inventory information with other municipal information systems. With these capacities in place, an inventory maintained primarily to support arboricultural operations can also become a tool for monitoring the urban forest and informing longer-term decisions.

Best practices for tree inventories

Today’s tree inventory best practices differ from those of decades prior. Based on the Canadian Urban Forest Census results, several practices can help municipalities maintain useful inventories and extend their value beyond day-to-day arboricultural operations.

  • Adopt digital data collection and storage.
  • Use geographic coordinates as a stable spatial reference.
  • Maintain accepted scientific names alongside common species names.
  • Implement version control or routine archiving to enable monitoring over time.
  • Seek out training opportunities to expand the uses of tree inventory data.
  • Consider publicly available canopy cover data to reduce the costs of canopy mapping.

These practices can extend the value of municipal inventories beyond their immediate operational uses and support long-term monitoring, comparisons among municipalities, and urban forestry research.
