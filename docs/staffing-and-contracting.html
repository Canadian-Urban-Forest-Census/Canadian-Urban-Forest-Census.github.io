Municipal urban forestry depends on a skilled workforce, from arborists caring for individual trees to urban foresters planning for the whole city, and on the contractors who carry out much of the planting, pruning, and removal. The 2025 Canadian Urban Forest Census asked municipalities how many people work in urban forestry, what training and credentials they hold, and who makes up the workforce, as well as what work they contract out and what standards and qualifications they expect contractors to bring. Which departments manage municipal trees is covered on the Management page, and contractor spending on the Urban Forest Budgets page.

We estimate that Canada’s 343 urban municipalities employ about 3,959 people in urban forestry, in 3,145 full-time equivalent (FTE) positions, and nearly all of them (97.1%) also use paid contractors. The typical municipality has five urban forestry staff. Most offer training and have credentialed staff, but access to credentials closely follows budget, and the workforce does not reflect the communities it serves. Removal and pruning are the most commonly contracted activities, and most municipalities give hiring preference to ISA Certified Arborists.

Staffing

Size of the workforce

Every responding municipality had at least one person working in urban forestry. The median municipality had 5 staff in 3.5 FTE positions, comparable to the median of 6 staff and 5 FTE positions in the United States. Street tree programs made up a large share of this workforce: a median of 3 staff and 1.2 FTE positions, or half of urban forestry staff and three-quarters of FTE positions.

Combining the reported numbers with model estimates for municipalities that did not respond gives a national picture of Canada’s municipal urban forestry workforce.

Larger municipalities, and those with more street tree canopy, had more urban forestry staff and FTE positions. For street tree programs, service demand mattered in a different way: municipalities receiving more street tree service requests were more likely to have dedicated street tree staff at all, but once a program existed, its size followed the extent of street tree canopy rather than the number of requests.

Training

Most municipalities provided in-house or on-the-job training (71.3%), and just over half had staff who attended tree care or management workshops (54.4%). In about one in five municipalities (19.9%), staff received neither. That is similar to the United States, where 70% of municipalities provide in-house training.

Credentials

Most municipalities (77.9%) had at least one staff member with an arboriculture or urban forestry credential, and the typical municipality’s staff held two different types between them. The ISA Certified Arborist credential was the most common, held by staff in 55.9% of municipalities, similar to the 59% reported in the United States. College diplomas in a related field (45.6%), pesticide applicator credentials (44.9%), and the ISA Tree Risk Assessment Qualification (41.9%) were also common.

Credentials closely followed money. Municipalities with larger urban forestry budgets were much more likely to have credentialed staff, and held a wider range of credentials, whereas the number of staff made no difference. Financial resources may be what allows municipalities to recruit, retain, and support staff with specialized qualifications.

Credentials required by provincial law

Some provinces require specific credentials for parts of urban forestry. In British Columbia, Ontario, and Québec, aspects of urban forestry fall under professional forestry regulation, while Manitoba and Saskatchewan require arborist credentials for prescribed pruning work. Few municipalities in the first three provinces had a Registered Professional Forester on staff. Municipalities in Manitoba and Saskatchewan were far more likely to have the credential their province requires.

Who works in urban forestry

Across responding municipalities, an estimated 68.3% of urban forestry staff were cisgender men, 14.1% were racialized persons, and 2.6% were First Nation, Inuit, or Métis persons. In the typical municipality, the workforce was 83% cisgender men, and most municipalities reported no racialized or Indigenous staff at all.

Compared with the communities they serve, most workforces were out of step. Cisgender men made up a larger share of staff than men did of the local labour force in 85.6% of municipalities. Racialized persons were underrepresented relative to the local population in 86.8% of municipalities, and First Nation, Inuit, or Métis persons in 88.0%.

Larger urban forestry programs tended to have more diverse workforces, with a lower share of cisgender men and higher shares of racialized and Indigenous staff. This may reflect the wider range of positions and recruitment opportunities in larger programs. Municipalities with larger Indigenous populations had more Indigenous staff, but generally not in proportion to the local population.

Underrepresentation can reinforce itself, shaping perceptions of who belongs in the profession. These figures provide a baseline for tracking change.

Contracting

Who uses contractors

Of the 136 municipalities that answered the question, 132 (97.1%) use paid contractors for at least some arboriculture activities, such as tree planting, pruning, or removal. Among those, 115 (87.1%) use contractors for work on street trees specifically. Contracting is therefore close to universal in Canadian urban forestry, and the more useful questions are what work is contracted and what is expected of the contractors who do it.

What contractors do

Among municipalities that contract street tree work, removal (92.2%) and pruning (84.3%) were by far the most commonly contracted activities. Just over half (58.3%) contracted tree planting, and just under half (47.0%) contracted plant health care, such as pesticide injections or spraying. Few municipalities (7.0%) used contractors for education and outreach.

Removal and pruning often involve large trees, specialized equipment, and significant risk, which may explain why they are contracted so widely. Larger municipalities were more likely to contract tree planting, and municipalities that planted more street trees were also more likely to contract that work.

What municipalities ask of contractors

Municipalities can set formal requirements for contracted work, such as following industry standards, and can give hiring preference to contractors with particular qualifications. Hiring preference for ISA Certified Arborists was by far the most common expectation, reported by 85.8% of municipalities that use contractors. Formal requirements to follow a work standard were less common: 41.6% required ISA Best Management Practices and 35.4% required ANSI A300 Standards.

Matching standards to the work

Planting-specific standards followed the planting work. Among municipalities that contract tree planting, 41.5% required contractors to use the Canadian Nursery Stock Standard, which sets specifications for the quality of nursery trees. In Québec, where the BNQ 0605-100 and 200 standards cover landscaping and tree maintenance work, every municipality that required BNQ standards also contracted tree planting.

General work standards, such as ISA Best Management Practices and ANSI A300, were required more often by municipalities that contract plant health care, which tend to contract a wider range of work overall. This suggests that some municipalities set more formal expectations across all of their contracts, rather than tailoring requirements to each activity.

Contract specifications are one of the main ways a municipality can shape the quality of contracted tree care. Naming the relevant standard for each type of work, such as a nursery stock standard for planting, makes those expectations explicit.
