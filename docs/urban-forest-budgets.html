Planting and caring for urban trees takes staff, equipment, nursery stock, and contracted services. The 2025 Canadian Urban Forest Census asked municipalities how much they budget for urban forestry, how much they spend on contractors, whether their budgets meet their needs, and how they use federal funding for tree planting.

We estimate that Canada’s 343 urban municipalities budget about $469.7 million a year for urban forestry and spend $222.4 million a year on contractors. Yet 60.3% of municipalities say their budget is not enough to meet current needs. Federal tree-planting funding has reached many municipalities, but mostly those that already have the staff to pursue it.

Budgets

Most municipalities (86.1%) had a budget for urban forestry, covering tree planting, maintenance, removal, staff, equipment, and contracts. The median budget was $250,000, or $36.89 for every municipally owned tree. Street tree programs received a median of 60% of the budget, and street tree planting a median of 10.7%.

Budgets were mostly growing. Between 2023 and 2024, excluding outside funding, 61.6% of municipalities increased their urban forestry budget, a third held it steady, and only 5.1% cut it. The median change was an increase of 3.0%.

What shapes budgets

Larger and denser municipalities had larger urban forestry budgets. Street tree spending also responded to demand: municipalities receiving more street tree service requests had larger street tree budgets and gave street trees a larger share of the overall budget. Municipalities with more canopy overall gave street trees a smaller share, likely because more of their budget goes to parks and natural areas.

Planting budgets showed the clearest effect of density. Denser municipalities spent more on street tree planting, both in total and per tree planted, which may reflect the cost of planting in tight urban spaces. Municipalities with more street tree canopy spent less on planting.

Economies of scale

Spending per tree fell as the amount of work grew. A municipality that plants twice as many street trees would be expected to have a planting budget only about 31% larger, and doubling the number of street trees planted, pruned, removed, or treated goes with a street tree budget only about 19% larger. Per-tree costs therefore depend heavily on the scale of a program and should not be compared between municipalities as a simple measure of efficiency.

Is the budget enough?

Only 39.7% of municipalities said their urban forestry budget was adequate to meet current needs, and 43.0% said the same of their street tree budget. Where budgets fell short, the median shortfall was 30% for urban forestry and 25% for street trees. Inadequate budgets were more common than in the United States, where 55% of municipalities reported an inadequate budget in 2025.

No municipal characteristic predicted whether a budget was considered adequate. Among municipalities with inadequate street tree budgets, larger municipalities reported smaller shortfalls, and those with more street tree canopy reported larger ones.

National spending and need

Filling the reported shortfalls would raise national urban forestry budgets from an estimated $469.7 million to $633.9 million a year, an increase of about $164 million or 35%.

Contractors

Almost every municipality (97.1%) paid contractors for some tree care, and 84.6% did so for street trees. The median municipality spent $60,000 on contractors, including $45,000 for street tree work, which made up a median of 88.8% of contractor spending. Nationally, we estimate municipalities spent $222.4 million on tree care contractors in 2024 (95% interval $178.1M–$344.6M), of which $191.2 million (86%) went to street trees.

Contracting grew roughly in step with internal capacity rather than replacing it: a 10% larger urban forestry budget went with about 9.5% more contractor spending, and 10% more FTE positions with about 10.9% more. Larger and denser municipalities, those with more street tree canopy, and those receiving more street tree service requests spent more on street tree contractors. More on what contractors do, and the standards municipalities set for them, is on the Staffing and Contracting page.

Federal funding for tree planting

Two federal programs fund municipal tree planting: the 2 Billion Trees program and Growing Canada’s Community Canopies, administered by the Federation of Canadian Municipalities. More than two-thirds of municipalities (69.3%) knew of at least one, and 46.7% knew of both. Nearly a third (30.7%) knew of neither.

Among municipalities that knew of the programs, 60.0% had applied, most often to Growing Canada’s Community Canopies. Of those that applied, 33 had received funding, 19 were still waiting for a decision, and only 5 had been turned down. The funded trees were mostly destined for parks (90.9%) and natural areas (72.7%), with fewer for streets (63.6%). No municipality reported that its own budget was cut because it received federal funding.

Slightly more than half of applicants (55.4%) said the funding would let them continue, improve, or complete programs that had been underfunded, while 44.6% said it would support new initiatives.

Who gets access

Federal funding does not appear to reach municipalities according to need. Municipalities with larger budgets and more staff were more aware of the programs, and those with more staff were more likely to apply. Whether a municipality considered its budget adequate made no difference to whether it applied. Finding, writing, and managing grant applications takes staff time, so municipalities with the least capacity may be the least able to use funding meant to build it.

Funding programs meant to build municipal capacity should consider the capacity needed to apply for them.
